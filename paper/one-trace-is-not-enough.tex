	\begin{abstract}
		Agentic coding benchmarks grade a candidate artifact by executing it and
		scoring the resulting trace: tests pass or fail, a task succeeds or does
		not, a judge reads a trajectory. We show that this evaluation model is
		structurally unable to express a large class of requirements we actually
		place on agent-authored systems. Properties such as calibrated
		conservatism under volatility, recency sensitivity, anti-windup,
		reproducibility across process restarts, and telemetry schema stability
		are \emph{hyperproperties}: predicates over sets of traces rather than
		over individual traces. No single execution can falsify them.
		
		We make three contributions. First, we give a property taxonomy for
		agentic evaluation grounded in the hyperproperty framework and audit a
		long-horizon control-system specification with twenty-two stated
		invariants. Seven invariants written in single-trace prose are only
		falsifiable as $2$-safety hyperproperties, so a conforming single-trace
		harness would silently pass artifacts that violate them. We prove the
		obstruction: for a stateful deterministic artifact, any asymmetry
		estimate drawn from one trace confounds the effect under test with
		internal state drift between the compared ticks.
		
		Second, we present \sys{}, a grading harness that renders every
		hyperproperty judgment on a coupled pair of executions: eight
		perturbation operators that vary one semantic dimension while holding
		exogenous input bit-identical, a coupling discipline that keeps
		closed-loop pairs comparable, and a \emph{probe tree} execution strategy
		that amortizes a shared prefix across branching perturbations.
		
		Third, we give a full evaluation protocol with synthetic positive and
		negative controls, a separation metric for distinguishing artifacts that
		trace-property grading ranks as equal, and a cost model. We report the
		protocol, handwritten controls, and offline harness coverage
		(green$\times$8$\times$3 via \texttt{gate\_axis\_mutate}); the model study
		is in progress and no accuracy, AUROC, or ranking scores are claimed here.
	\end{abstract}

	\section{Introduction}
	
	The dominant evaluation model for agent-authored software is trace scoring.
	A benchmark supplies an input, the artifact runs, and a grader inspects what
	came out: a test suite verdict, a task completion flag, a reward from a
	simulated environment, or a language-model judgment over a recorded
	trajectory. The unit of judgment is one execution.
	
	This model is adequate when the requirement is itself a property of one
	execution. ``The function returns the correct value'' and ``the agent
	eventually books the flight'' are both decided by watching a single run.
	Much of what we want from autonomous systems is not of this form. Consider
	four requirements drawn verbatim from an industrial specification for a
	fleet traffic controller:
	
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Asymmetric response.} The controller's downward correction to
		a deviation from setpoint must strictly exceed twice its upward correction
		to a deviation of equal magnitude and opposite sign.
		\item \textbf{Volatility suppression.} Given two workloads with equal mean
		and different variance, the controller must converge to a strictly more
		conservative operating point on the higher-variance workload.
		\item \textbf{Anti-windup.} After sustained saturation at a ceiling, the
		first post-saturation response must be bounded by the immediately observed
		error and not by the accumulated saturation window.
		\item \textbf{Determinism.} The same configuration and observation sequence
		must yield a bit-identical decision sequence across fresh interpreter
		processes.
	\end{itemize}
	
	None of these can be falsified by examining one trace. Each is a statement
	relating two or more executions. Determinism is the textbook case: it is
	the observational-determinism hyperproperty from the information-flow
	literature, and it is well understood there that it is not a trace property
	\cite{clarkson2010,zdancewic2003}. Our claim is that the other three are
	formally the same kind of object, that this class is large, and that it is
	precisely the class capturing the judgment and calibration we most want to
	measure in agent-authored systems.
	
	The practical consequence is a silent failure mode in benchmark design. A
	specification author writes an invariant in single-trace prose. A harness
	author implements a single-trace check that appears to match the prose. The
	check passes on artifacts that violate the intent, because the discriminating
	evidence never appears in one run. The benchmark reports a score, the score
	is wrong, and nothing in the pipeline signals the gap.
	
	\subsection{Contributions}
	
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item \textbf{A property taxonomy for agentic evaluation} (Section
		\ref{sec:taxonomy}). We classify grading targets into trace safety,
		quantitative trace properties, $k$-safety hyperproperties, quantitative
		hyperproperties, and resource properties, and we state for each what
		evidence is required to falsify it.
		
		\item \textbf{An audit of a real specification} (Section \ref{sec:audit}).
		We classify all twenty-two invariants of a long-horizon orchestrator task.
		Seven are misclassified in the source specification in the direction that
		matters: they read as single-trace checks but are only falsifiable with a
		pair. We also find that the specification's stated ``hardest to satisfy''
		class and its hardest-to-\emph{check} class are disjoint.
		
		\item \textbf{A state-confounding obstruction} (Proposition
		\ref{prop:confound}). For a deterministic stateful transducer on a
		non-repeating observation stream, no single trace supplies both arms of the
		counterfactual contrast that an asymmetry invariant requires. Paired
		execution from a shared state is not a convenience; it is the only
		construction that identifies the quantity.
		
		\item \textbf{\sys{}, a paired-trace grading harness} (Sections
		\ref{sec:harness} and \ref{sec:operators}). Eight perturbation operators, a
		coupling discipline for closed-loop simulators based on common random
		numbers, a validity check that detects when a pair has drifted out of
		comparability, and a probe-tree execution strategy with an amortized cost
		model (Proposition \ref{prop:prefix}).
		
		\item \textbf{An evaluation protocol with ground-truth controls} (Section
		\ref{sec:protocol}). Hand-written artifacts that satisfy or violate each
		hyperproperty by construction, used to establish probe power before any
		model is scored. The same section places product adapters ZeroDay and
		AOMB at pinned SHAs and records offline harness facts
		(\texttt{gate\_axis\_mutate}, green$\times$8$\times$3 coverage cells) as
		protocol / QC evidence only---not model scores, accuracy, or
		vulnerability findings.
	\end{enumerate}
	
	We are explicit about scope. This paper delivers the formalism, the audit,
	the harness design, and the protocol. The model study is in progress.
	Section \ref{sec:protocol} states the hypotheses and the measurement plan;
	it reports no model scores. Offline harness and adapter-pin facts are
	cited only as protocol evidence.
	
	\section{Motivating Example}
	\label{sec:motivating}
	
	We ground the paper in a concrete long-horizon task, which we also use as
	the audit subject and the harness testbed.
	
	\subsection{The orchestrator task}
	
	An agent is asked to author a single Python component, an \emph{adaptive
		traffic orchestrator}, that observes a simulated distributed fleet and
	returns control decisions each tick. The public contract is three methods:
	a constructor from configuration, a \texttt{tick} method mapping an
	observation to a decision bundle, and a state serialization pair used to
	kill and restore the process. Internal structure is entirely the agent's
	choice.
	
	Each observation reports per-endpoint incoming request rate by priority,
	recent latency samples, error rate, queue depth, in-flight count, capacity,
	heartbeat gap, and ejection status, together with a service topology that
	may change between ticks and a set of operator directives such as a latency
	objective. Each decision may set admission verdicts per priority, shed
	rates, routing targets, concurrency limits, timeouts, rate limits,
	ejections, replica deltas, alerts, and telemetry events.
	
	Grading runs the artifact through fifteen sealed adversarial scenarios
	spanning steady state, diurnal load, regional brownout, silent partition,
	retry storms, cascade attempts, weight changes under load, setpoint hunting,
	heavy-tail pressure, correlated latency spikes, flapping endpoints, node
	rollout, and a full serialization-and-restart cycle. The scenarios are
	scored against twenty-two stated invariants.
	
	The task is a good testbed for three reasons. It is long-horizon, with
	scenarios running to millions of simulated ticks. It is deterministic by
	construction, which is a precondition for paired execution. And its
	invariants were written by systems practitioners rather than by formal
	methods researchers, so the classification errors we find are the errors a
	realistic specification actually contains.
	
	\subsection{Two orchestrators a trace grader cannot separate}
	\label{sec:twoorch}
	
	Consider two candidate artifacts. Both maintain an exponentially weighted
	estimate of endpoint latency and adjust a concurrency limit toward a
	setpoint. $A_{\mathrm{sym}}$ applies gain $g$ to the error in both
	directions. $A_{\mathrm{asym}}$ applies $g_{\uparrow}$ when the observed
	latency is below setpoint and $g_{\downarrow}$ when it is above, with
	$g_{\downarrow} > 2 g_{\uparrow}$ and $g_{\uparrow} + g_{\downarrow} = 2g$.
	Only $A_{\mathrm{asym}}$ satisfies the asymmetric-response invariant.
	
	Under a workload whose deviations from setpoint are symmetric in
	distribution, the two artifacts produce traces with matching totals: the
	same mean concurrency limit, the same cumulative admitted load, comparable
	objective attainment, comparable cost. A grader scoring any aggregate of a
	single trace sees two equally good artifacts.
	
	One might object that a single long trace contains both upward and downward
	deviations, so the ratio could be read off directly. It cannot, and the
	reason is the technical heart of the paper. The response to an upward
	deviation at tick $t$ and to a downward deviation at tick $t'$ are responses
	\emph{from different internal states}. The estimator has moved, the
	saturation history differs, the topology may have changed. Any ratio
	computed across those two moments mixes the sign effect with state drift.
	Proposition \ref{prop:confound} formalizes this. The only construction that
	identifies the sign effect holds the state fixed, which means branching two
	executions from a common prefix.

	\section{A Property Taxonomy for Agentic Evaluation}
	\label{sec:taxonomy}
	
	\subsection{Setup}
	
	We model an artifact as a deterministic transducer
	$A = (S, s_0, \delta, \lambda)$ over an observation alphabet $O$ and a
	decision alphabet $D$, with transition $\delta : S \times O \to S$ and
	output $\lambda : S \times O \to D$. Determinism is a requirement of the
	task, not an assumption of convenience; where an artifact is stochastic we
	take the seed to be part of the configuration.
	
	An execution on an observation sequence $\bar{o} = o_1 \ldots o_n$ yields a
	trace
	\[
	T = \exec(A, c, \bar{o}) = \langle (o_1, d_1), \ldots, (o_n, d_n) \rangle ,
	\]
	with $s_i = \delta(s_{i-1}, o_i)$ and $d_i = \lambda(s_{i-1}, o_i)$. Write
	$\Tr(A, c)$ for the set of traces $A$ produces over the workload family of
	interest.
	
	\begin{definition}[Trace property]
		A trace property is a set $P$ of traces. Artifact $A$ satisfies $P$ iff
		$\Tr(A,c) \subseteq P$.
	\end{definition}
	
	\begin{definition}[Hyperproperty]
		A hyperproperty is a set $H$ of \emph{sets} of traces. Artifact $A$
		satisfies $H$ iff $\Tr(A,c) \in H$ \cite{clarkson2010}.
	\end{definition}
	
	\begin{definition}[$k$-safety]
		A hyperproperty is $k$-safety if every violation is witnessed by a set of at
		most $k$ traces. A $k$-safety hyperproperty is decided by $k$-fold self
		composition: execute the artifact $k$ times on related inputs and evaluate a
		predicate over the resulting tuple \cite{barthe2004,terauchi2005}.
	\end{definition}
	
	The practical reading of $k$-safety is a cost statement. A $1$-safety
	requirement needs one run. A $2$-safety requirement needs a matched pair,
	and the matching is where the engineering lies.
	
	\subsection{The five classes}
	
	\textbf{Trace safety.} A bad prefix exists. One run falsifies. Example: an
	ejection set that drops a rack below its live-endpoint floor. These are
	cheap and are what current harnesses check well.
	
	\textbf{Quantitative trace properties.} A real-valued functional of one
	trace compared against a threshold. One long run falsifies, subject to
	sampling error. Example: realized admitted-load share per priority within
	tolerance of the configured weights.
	
	\textbf{$k$-safety hyperproperties.} A tuple of $k$ traces falsifies; no
	single trace does. Almost everything in this paper is $k=2$. Example:
	asymmetric response, determinism, state round-trip stability.
	
	\textbf{Quantitative hyperproperties.} A comparator over a tuple of traces,
	with a threshold, rather than a boolean. Example: volatility suppression
	requires that the conservatism of the high-variance arm strictly dominate
	that of the low-variance arm, which is a real-valued contrast over a pair.
	
	\textbf{Resource properties.} Statements about execution cost rather than
	output. Example: median tick latency at reference scale, state size bounded
	by topology and independent of tick count. These are single-trace but noisy,
	and require repetition for a different reason than $k$-safety does.

	Three findings follow.
	
	\textbf{Seven invariants are misclassified, all in the unsafe direction.}
	Each starred row reads as a check a harness author would implement against a
	single trace, and each is only falsifiable with a pair. Three of the seven
	are not even framed comparatively in the source text. Anti-windup says the
	post-saturation response is bounded by the immediate error ``and not by the
	accumulated saturation window.'' A single trace cannot separate a controller
	that respects this from one that happened to accumulate little. The
	falsifying evidence is a pair of executions that share an immediate error
	and differ in saturation duration. Setpoint-change quiet has the same
	shape, with pre-step history as the varied dimension. Telemetry schema
	stability quantifies over all emissions in all scenarios, so a violation is
	witnessed by two emissions that may live in different traces.
	
	\textbf{Hard to satisfy and hard to check are different axes.} The
	specification names its coherence group as the class that surfaces design
	flaws, the one requiring the author to revisit early decisions. Every
	coherence invariant is nonetheless plain trace safety: the double-tightening
	check inspects one tick, the failover check inspects one tick, the alert
	checks inspect a bounded window. They are difficult to \emph{satisfy} and
	trivial to \emph{detect}. The adaptive-control group is the inverse. This
	distinction is worth making explicit for benchmark authors, because effort
	on harness construction should follow checkability, not difficulty.
	
	\textbf{The misclassification is systematic, not careless.} Six of the seven
	starred invariants concern calibration: how strongly to react, how much to
	weight recent evidence, how conservative to be under uncertainty. Calibration
	is inherently a statement about how behavior would differ under a
	counterfactual input, and counterfactual contrasts are not single-trace
	objects. We expect this pattern to recur in any specification that asks for
	judgment rather than for conformance.
	
	\subsection{The obstruction}
	
	\begin{proposition}[State confounding]
		\label{prop:confound}
		Let $A$ be a deterministic transducer and let $T = \exec(A, c, \bar{o})$ be a
		trace in which no state recurs, that is $s_i \neq s_j$ for $i \neq j$.
		Define the counterfactual sign contrast at state $s$ and base observation
		$o$ as
		\[
		\rho(s, o, e) \;=\; \big\| \lambda(s, o \oplus e) - \lambda(s, o) \big\|
		\;-\; \big\| \lambda(s, o \ominus e) - \lambda(s, o) \big\| ,
		\]
		where $\oplus$ and $\ominus$ perturb the observation by $+e$ and $-e$. Then
		$T$ contains at most one of the two terms of $\rho$ for any state it visits,
		and every estimator of $\rho$ computed from $T$ alone is a contrast between
		outputs at two distinct states $s_i \neq s_j$, hence confounds the sign
		effect with the state difference $s_i \to s_j$.
	\end{proposition}
	
	\begin{IEEEproof}[Proof sketch]
		Fix a tick $i$. The trace records the single pair $(o_i, d_i)$ with
		$d_i = \lambda(s_{i-1}, o_i)$. Evaluating $\rho(s_{i-1}, o_i, e)$ requires
		$\lambda(s_{i-1}, \cdot)$ at two further arguments $o_i \oplus e$ and
		$o_i \ominus e$, neither of which the trace supplies, because the execution
		proceeded from $s_{i-1}$ on exactly one input. Any substitute drawn from the
		same trace uses $\lambda(s_{j-1}, \cdot)$ for some $j \neq i$, and by the
		no-recurrence hypothesis $s_{j-1} \neq s_{i-1}$. The resulting quantity is
		therefore a sum of the sign effect and the state effect, and no assumption
		available to the grader separates them. Identification requires evaluating
		$\lambda$ at a common state, which is achieved by executing the shared
		prefix once and branching.
	\end{IEEEproof}
	
	The no-recurrence hypothesis is mild for the systems we care about. A
	long-horizon controller carries real-valued estimators over a
	non-stationary observation stream; exact state recurrence is a measure-zero
	event. Where an artifact does have a small recurrent state space, the
	proposition weakens gracefully: identification becomes possible but requires
	the grader to detect recurrence, which requires state inspection the public
	contract does not permit.
	
	\section{\sys{}: Paired-Trace Grading}
	\label{sec:harness}
	
	\sys{} renders every hyperproperty judgment on two panels. Its design
	addresses three problems: constructing a valid pair, keeping the pair
	comparable in a closed loop, and paying for the extra executions.
	
	\subsection{Coupling discipline}
	
	\begin{definition}[Exogenous coupling]
		Two executions are exogenously coupled if every input not causally derived
		from the artifact's own decisions is bit-identical between them, except for
		the single dimension the perturbation operator varies.
	\end{definition}
	
	The harness encodes coupling as an envelope enum
	\texttt{coupling}$\,\in\,\{\texttt{open\_loop},\,\texttt{crn\_closed\_loop}\}$
	(\texttt{diptych.COUPLINGS}; enforced by \texttt{ops/*/spec.yaml} and the
	schema-\texttt{0.2} contract). The choice is dictated by what is being
	measured, not by convenience.
	
	\begin{definition}[Open-loop coupling]
		\label{def:openloop}
		A probe is \texttt{open\_loop} if both arms are driven by a fixed
		observation (or cassette) sequence that does not depend on the artifact's
		decisions after the shared prefix. Decisions are recorded and graded but
		do not feed the plant. Coupling is therefore exact: the only intentional
		difference between arms is the operator axis.
	\end{definition}
	
	Open-loop probing isolates the response function in Proposition
	\ref{prop:confound}. It is the harness default for
	\textsc{FreezeDry}, \textsc{Reseed}, \textsc{SchemaX}, \textsc{SignFlip},
	\textsc{SatExtend}, and \textsc{HistSwap}---statements about response
	magnitude, fingerprint stability, or schema identity under a controlled
	axis.
	
	\begin{definition}[CRN closed-loop coupling]
		\label{def:crn}
		A probe is \texttt{crn\_closed\_loop} if both arms run against a live
		plant under a shared common-random-numbers (CRN) stream
		(\texttt{meta.crn\_stream\_id}, \texttt{meta.crn\_closed\_loop=true} on
		each twin). Exogenous noise, arrivals, and fault injections are drawn
		from the shared stream; arms diverge only through the perturbation and
		the artifact's closed-loop reaction. The contract requires this enum for
		\textsc{TrajSwap} and \textsc{VarScale} (\texttt{diptych.CRN\_REQUIRED}).
	\end{definition}
	
	CRN closed-loop probing measures outcomes---which operating point the system
	settles into under mean-matched variance or swapped trajectory segments---
	rather than open-loop response magnitude. Residual channels
	(\texttt{channels.closed\_loop\_residual}, variance-proxy ordering) are the
	graded witnesses; open-loop tagging of those operators is a contract reject.
	
	\subsection{Comparability horizon}
	
	A closed-loop pair eventually becomes incomparable. Once the two plants have
	drifted far apart, a difference in decisions no longer attributes to the
	perturbation. \sys{} therefore attaches to each probe a
	\emph{comparability horizon} declared on the envelope and mirrored in
	\texttt{ops/*/spec.yaml}.
	
	\begin{definition}[Comparability horizon]
		\label{def:horizon}
		The comparability horizon of a probe is the envelope field
		\texttt{horizon}$=\{$\texttt{unit},\,\texttt{length}$\}$, where
		\texttt{unit}$\,\in\,\{\texttt{steps},\,\texttt{ms},\,\texttt{events}\}$
		and \texttt{length} is a non-negative integer. A twin pair remains
		\emph{comparable} for grading only while a declared divergence statistic
		(for CRN probes: closed-loop residual / plant-state distance; for
		open-loop probes: shared-prefix integrity) stays inside the operator's
		envelope for the full horizon window.
	\end{definition}
	
	When the horizon check fails---shared-prefix breakage, missing twin role, or
	residual outside bound---the harness does not invent a pass or fail. It
	emits an inconclusive verdict (Definition~\ref{def:inconclusive}).
	
	\subsection{Inconclusive verdicts}
	
	\begin{definition}[Inconclusive verdict]
		\label{def:inconclusive}
		Schema~\texttt{0.2} admits exactly three expected verdicts:
		\texttt{pass}, \texttt{fail}, and \texttt{inconclusive}
		(\texttt{diptych.VERDICTS}). An envelope may declare
		\texttt{expected\_verdict=inconclusive} only with
		\texttt{meta.inconclusive\_reason}. Operator specs list
		\texttt{inconclusive\_when} conditions (shared-prefix incomparability,
		missing twin role). Coverage counting treats
		\texttt{inconclusive}$\,\neq\,$green: a cell is green only under
		conforming$\to$\texttt{pass}, violating$\to$\texttt{fail}, and
		\texttt{gate\_axis\_mutate} power (\texttt{docs/adapters/GATING.md}).
	\end{definition}
	
	Reporting inconclusive separately is deliberate. Silent degradation of a
	hyperproperty probe into a coin flip is exactly the failure mode the paper
	argues against; a harness that hides incomparability reproduces the problem
	one level up. We do not convert inconclusive rates into model scores.
	
	\subsection{Probe trees and amortization}
	
	Naive $k$-safety grading multiplies cost by $k$. Deterministic replay makes
	that unnecessary. If a perturbation is applied at tick $t_p$, both arms
	share the prefix $o_1 \ldots o_{t_p}$ exactly, so the prefix need only be
	executed once. The formulas below are a \emph{protocol cost model}: they
	define how the harness accounts for tick evaluations. They are not measured
	wall-clock results and carry no claimed AUROC or dollar cost.
	
	\begin{proposition}[Prefix sharing]
		\label{prop:prefix}
		For a $2$-safety probe with perturbation point $t_p$ and scenario length
		$n$, exogenously coupled execution costs $n + (n - t_p)$ tick evaluations
		rather than $2n$.
	\end{proposition}
	
	\begin{corollary}[Probe tree]
		\label{cor:tree}
		Let $m$ probes share a perturbation point $t_p$. Executing them as a tree
		rooted at the shared prefix costs
		$t_p + \sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(n_j - t_p\right)$ rather than
		$\sum_{j=1}^{m} 2 n_j$. For probes with late perturbation points the
		amortized marginal cost of an additional probe approaches the length of its
		suffix alone.
	\end{corollary}
	
	\begin{definition}[Probe-tree cost (protocol)]
		\label{def:cost}
		Let $C_{\mathrm{naive}}(m)=\sum_{j=1}^{m} 2 n_j$ and
		$C_{\mathrm{tree}}(m)=t_p + \sum_{j=1}^{m}(n_j-t_p)$ under a shared
		fork at $t_p$. The protocol amortization factor is
		$\alpha(m)=C_{\mathrm{naive}}(m)/C_{\mathrm{tree}}(m)$ when
		$C_{\mathrm{tree}}(m)>0$. Reporting $\alpha$ in a model study requires
		measured tick counts; until then the factor is definitional only.
	\end{definition}
	
	The engineering requirement is a cheap fork of artifact state at $t_p$. The
	orchestrator contract already provides one, since the task mandates a
	serialize-and-restore pair with bit-identical subsequent behavior
	(\textsc{FreezeDry}'s graded axis). This is a pleasant alignment: the same
	property that makes the artifact operationally restartable makes
	hyperproperty grading affordable. We suggest that future task specifications
	require a state fork for this reason, independent of any operational
	motivation.
	
	\subsection{Power-on-axis: \texttt{gate\_axis\_mutate}}
	
	Twin contrast alone is not enough: a harness can ship identical twins with
	hard-coded opposite \texttt{expected\_verdict} labels, or rename a cosmetic
	field, and still look green. \sys{} therefore requires
	\texttt{gate\_axis\_mutate}: on each conforming twin, mutate \emph{only} the
	operator's graded axis (seed, freeze mask, schema key, polarity channel,
	saturation bound, history splice, trajectory segment, or variance scale) and
	demand that the grader flip \texttt{pass}$\to$\texttt{fail}. Cosmetic edits
	--- flipping the expected verdict alone, renaming a SARIF level, or injecting
	an AUROC field --- leave the axis fingerprint unchanged and fail the gate.
	A coverage cell is green only when conforming$\to$\texttt{pass},
	violating$\to$\texttt{fail}, and \texttt{gate\_axis\_mutate} reports power.

	Preservation intent (exogenous identity except the varied axis) is enforced by
	the coupling enum and graded fields above; two operators deserve comment
	because their construction is subtle.
	
	\textsc{TrajSwap} must hold aggregate statistics fixed while changing the
	recent trajectory. We construct the twin by permuting the samples inside a
	comparison window, which preserves the multiset exactly and therefore
	preserves every order-insensitive statistic, then choosing the two
	permutations so that one window ends in improvement and the other ends in
	degradation. An artifact that summarizes history with an order-insensitive
	statistic produces identical decisions on both arms and fails the probe by
	construction, which is the intended discrimination.
	
	\textsc{SatExtend} must hold the immediately observed error fixed while
	varying how long the artifact sat against a ceiling. We construct the twin
	by extending the saturated segment and splicing an identical release
	segment onto both arms. The comparator reads the first post-release response
	magnitude. An artifact with an unbounded integrator responds more strongly
	on the longer arm, which is the anti-windup violation stated in prose in the
	source specification and, as Section \ref{sec:audit} notes, not detectable
	without this pair.
	
	\textsc{SchemaX} is the one operator that is not a twin of a single
	scenario. It compares emission records of a common event type across two
	different scenarios, which makes it $2$-safety over the trace set rather
	than over a perturbation pair. We include it in the same framework because
	the falsification structure is identical: two traces, one predicate, no
	single-run witness.
	
	\section{Metrics}
	\label{sec:metrics}
	
	\subsection{Probe power}
	
	A probe is useless if it never fires on a violating artifact or always fires
	on a conforming one. For each operator we define power against the control
	artifacts of Section \ref{sec:protocol}: the fraction of violating controls
	on which the probe returns a fail, and the fraction of conforming controls
	on which it returns a pass. Probes that do not achieve a declared power
	floor against ground truth are excluded from scoring rather than reported
	with a caveat.
	
	\subsection{Separation index}
	
	The central claim of the paper is that hyperproperty grading distinguishes
	artifacts that trace-property grading does not. We measure this directly.
	For a pair of artifacts $A$ and $B$, let $\mathrm{eq}_{\mathrm{tr}}(A,B)$
	hold when their trace-property score vectors agree within tolerance. The
	separation index is the fraction of such trace-equivalent pairs on which the
	hyperproperty score vectors disagree. An index near zero refutes the paper's
	premise; an index near one establishes that hyperproperty probes carry
	information that no amount of trace grading recovers.
	
	\subsection{Predictive validity}
	
	Discrimination is not automatically useful. We therefore hold out a subset
	of adversarial scenarios, score artifacts on hyperproperty probes derived
	only from the training scenarios, and measure rank correlation between probe
	score and held-out outcome quality. The comparison baseline is the same
	correlation computed from trace-property scores alone. The hypothesis is
	that hyperproperty probes predict held-out robustness better, because
	calibration generalizes across scenarios while scenario-specific outcome
	tuning does not.
	
	\subsection{Cost}
	
	The probe-tree formulas (Proposition~\ref{prop:prefix},
	Corollary~\ref{cor:tree}, Definition~\ref{def:cost}) are a \emph{protocol
	cost model}. A future model study may report measured tick evaluations per
	probe against the $2n$ naive baseline and the realized amortization factor
	$\alpha$; those measurements are not claimed here and must not be invented.
	
	\section{Evaluation Protocol}
	\label{sec:protocol}
	
	This section states the study we are running and the offline harness
	protocol that precedes it. It reports \emph{no model scores}.
	
	\subsection{Ground-truth controls}
	
	Before any model artifact is scored, every probe is validated against
	hand-written controls whose status is known by construction. For each of the
	six behavioral hyperproperties we write a conforming controller and a
	violating one differing in a single design decision: symmetric versus
	asymmetric gain; order-sensitive versus order-insensitive history summary;
	variance-aware versus variance-blind headroom; clamped versus unclamped
	integrator; history-dependent versus history-independent step response;
	stable versus drifting telemetry schema. A probe that cannot separate its
	own control pair is broken, and we would rather discover that before
	interpreting a model score than after.
	
	Controls also calibrate tolerance. The quantitative comparators need a
	threshold, and we set each one at the midpoint between the contrast measured
	on the conforming control and the contrast measured on the violating one,
	rather than by intuition.

	\section{Related Work}
	\label{sec:related}
	
	\textbf{Hyperproperties and relational verification.} The hyperproperty
	framework and the safety-liveness classification over trace sets are due to
	Clarkson and Schneider \cite{clarkson2010}; temporal logics (HyperLTL /
	HyperCTL$^*$) follow \cite{clarkson2014}, with model-checking algorithms in
	\cite{finkbeiner2015} and related information-flow checking in
	\cite{dimitrova2014}. Observational determinism as a security condition
	predates the general framework \cite{roscoe1995,zdancewic2003}. Self
	composition reduces $k$-safety verification to ordinary safety verification
	of a product program \cite{barthe2004,terauchi2005}. Our contribution is not
	to that theory. It is the observation that agentic benchmark design has an
	unrecognized dependence on it, and a grading harness that acts on pairs.
	
	\textbf{Observational equivalence and differential privacy.} Differential
	privacy calibrates distinguishability over neighboring datasets
	\cite{dwork2006,dwork2014}: the object of judgment is again a relation over
	executions (or query answers), not a single transcript. We share the
	\emph{relational} stance---grading requires comparing related runs---but we
	target calibration and reproducibility hyperproperties of agent-authored
	controllers, not privacy budgets or $(\varepsilon,\delta)$ guarantees. We do
	not claim a privacy result.
	
	\textbf{Runtime verification and control monitors.} Runtime verification
	monitors properties of observed traces \cite{leucker2009,bartocci2018}, with
	tool taxonomies in \cite{falcone2018}. Classic RV is powerful for
	single-trace temporal properties; $k$-safety hyperproperties still require
	multiple runs (or an explicit product construction). \sys{} is closer to a
	paired metamorphic harness than to an online monitor: we deliberately
	perturb one axis and compare twins under a coupling discipline.
	
	\textbf{Metamorphic testing.} Metamorphic relations test a program by
	relating outputs on related inputs when no oracle is available
	\cite{chen1998,segura2016}. Every metamorphic relation is a $k$-safety
	hyperproperty, so the two literatures describe overlapping objects with
	different vocabularies. The distinction here is the subject and the target:
	we test a stateful long-horizon artifact \emph{authored by} an agent,
	against calibration requirements, under a closed-loop simulator that makes
	input coupling nontrivial. Coupling discipline and comparability horizons for
	closed-loop paired grading are the engineering gap we address.
	
	\textbf{Agentic and LLM evaluation (single-trace grading).} Contemporary
	benchmarks score repository-level task completion, tool-use trajectories,
	web or multi-turn reward, or unit-test pass rates
	\cite{jimenez2024,yao2024,liu2023,zhou2024,chen2021}. All of these grade
	single executions (or aggregates of independent single executions).
	Trajectory judging inherits the same limitation regardless of judge quality:
	the counterfactual evidence a hyperproperty needs is not in one trajectory.
	We position \sys{} as complementary, not as a replacement pass@k leaderboard:
	where the requirement is calibration across related worlds, one trace is not
	enough. We report no invented AUROC, F1, or model scores against those
	benchmarks.
	
	\textbf{Differential and regression testing.} Differential testing compares
	independent implementations on shared inputs \cite{mckeeman1998}. We compare
	one implementation on deliberately related inputs, which is the dual
	construction, and we require the relation between inputs to be semantically
	controlled rather than merely shared.
	
	\section{Threats to Validity}
	\label{sec:threats}
	
	\textbf{Construct.} Our operators encode one reading of each invariant's
	intent. A different reading yields a different twin and possibly a different
	verdict. We mitigate by publishing operators as executable code alongside the
	prose (Table~\ref{tab:ops}), so disagreement about intent becomes disagreement
	about a specific artifact rather than about an interpretation. ``Green'' means
	twin contrast plus \texttt{gate\_axis\_mutate} under schema~\texttt{0.2}
	(Figure~\ref{fig:diptych}), not exploitability, deployment certification, or
	model quality. Graded channels are contract fields, not AUROC or
	\texttt{model\_grade} scores (rejected by the envelope contract).
	
	\textbf{Adapter pin drift.} ZeroDay and AOMB columns are greens at pinned SHAs
	only (Section~\ref{sec:adapters}, Figure~\ref{fig:stack},
	\texttt{adapters/PINS.md}). A silent pin move, emitter schema skew, or
	cassette-format change without a matching QC can flip adapter cells while
	\texttt{diptych\_core} stays green. We treat pin/SHA mismatch as a
	reproducibility threat: product trees are not vendored here, so reviewers
	must re-resolve pins rather than assume floating \texttt{main}.
	
	\textbf{Inconclusive verdicts.} Schema~\texttt{0.2} admits
	\texttt{inconclusive} with \texttt{meta.inconclusive\_reason} when twins are
	incomparable (horizon / shared-prefix failure, missing twin role). That
	verdict is \emph{not} green and does not count toward coverage. Over-use of
	inconclusive can hide weak probes; under-use can force spurious pass/fail on
	incomparable pairs. Gating docs make the counting rule explicit; we do not
	convert inconclusive rates into model scores.
	
	\textbf{Handwritten-control bias.} Conforming and violating controls establish
	probe power before any model is scored. Controls can overfit the grader
	(passing only the handwritten shape) or understate difficulty for agent-authored
	artifacts. Mitigations are public control sources under \texttt{controls/},
	axis mutation that must flip pass$\rightarrow$fail, and a held-out model study
	that is still in progress---so Table~\ref{tab:placeholder} stays blank rather
	than imputing power from controls alone.
	
	\textbf{Open-loop vs.\ CRN coupling limits.} Six operators use
	\texttt{open\_loop} exogenous coupling; \textsc{TrajSwap} and \textsc{VarScale}
	require \texttt{crn\_closed\_loop}. Open-loop twins do not exercise feedback
	confounding; CRN closed-loop twins can still drift out of comparability, and
	drift rate may correlate with artifact quality. RQ5 is designed to measure
	that explicitly rather than assuming it away. We do not claim that open-loop
	greens transfer to closed-loop deployments, nor that CRN residuals are a
	universal coupling for every simulator.
	
	\textbf{Internal.} Beyond coupling drift, grader bugs would systematically
	mislabel cells; the full-8 offline gate and matrix check against
	\texttt{examples/poc/expected\_matrix\_snippet.json} are the regression net
	for harness logic, not for model quality.
	
	\textbf{External.} Findings are drawn from one task family, a fleet traffic
	controller. The taxonomy should transfer to any agentic task whose
	requirements include calibration, but the specific operators will not. We
	claim generality for the classification and the harness architecture, not
	for the operator set.
	
	\textbf{Conclusion / non-claims.} Twin construction is deterministic, so
	classical significance testing over probe outcomes is inappropriate.
	Variation for the forthcoming model study enters through scenario sampling
	and artifact sampling only. We do not claim AUROC improvements, ranking
	lifts, or security exploit findings; Table~\ref{tab:placeholder} stays blank
	until that study completes. Localization $\neq$ exploitability;
	\texttt{inconclusive}$\neq$green (gating contract).
	
	\section{Conclusion}
	
	Agentic benchmarks grade one trace at a time, and a substantial class of the
	requirements we place on agent-authored systems cannot be falsified that
	way. We classified the grading targets of a real long-horizon control task
	and found that seven of its twenty-two invariants, including every invariant
	concerning calibration, are only falsifiable as $2$-safety hyperproperties,
	though most are written as if a single run would do. We proved the
	obstruction that makes this unavoidable for stateful artifacts, and we
	presented a harness that renders each such judgment on a coupled pair, with
	a coupling discipline for closed-loop simulators, an explicit
	inconclusive verdict, and a probe-tree cost model that makes paired grading
	affordable.
	
	The broader point is a design rule for benchmark authors. When a requirement
	concerns how strongly a system should react, how much it should weight
	recent evidence, or how conservative it should be under uncertainty, the
	requirement is a statement about a counterfactual, and a counterfactual
	needs two executions. Writing it as a single-trace check does not make it
	one.
